What is a credit union?
A credit union is a not-for-profit financial cooperative owned by the people it serves. When you become a Member, you gain access to financial products, guidance, and an equal voice in how the organization is led.
Money decisions can affect your next apartment, vehicle, home, business, or emergency plan. Yet many people choose a financial institution without knowing who owns it or where its earnings go. Understanding what a credit union is can help you choose a financial partner built around your goals.
Credit unions offer checking accounts, savings accounts, credit cards, and loans. Their cooperative structure creates the main difference. Members own the credit union instead of outside investors.
Member ownership helps keep financial well-being at the center of every service.
What Is a Credit Union and How Do They Work?
Credit unions pool Members deposits and use those funds to support loans and other financial services. Revenue may come from:
Loan interest
Service fees
Investments
Earnings can:
Strengthen the credit union
Improve services
Fund education
Create greater value for Members
Members elect a volunteer board of directors to guide the organization. Each Member usually receives one vote, regardless of the amount saved. Equal voting rights keep leadership connected to the people it serves.
You are not only an account holder. You are part of the cooperative.
Credit unions must also follow federal or state regulations. These rules cover areas such as:
Lending
Privacy
Disclosures
Financial safety
Regulation helps protect Members while supporting responsible operations.
Is there a Downside to Credit Unions?
Credit unions offer many valuable benefits, but their services, technology, and accessibility can vary greatly between organizations. Possible limits include:
Eligibility rules
Fewer physical locations
A smaller product range at some credit unions
Comparing access, rates, fees, and services can help you decide whether a credit union fits your routine.

What Makes a Credit Union Different?
The defining difference is simple.
A credit union exists to serve their Members. A shareholder-owned financial institution must also answer to outside investors.
Cooperative ownership allows a credit union to focus more closely on long-term Member value.
Common differences include
:
Members own and direct the organization.
Members elect the board of directors.
Earnings can support better value and service.
Eligibility follows a defined field of membership.
Community needs often shape outreach and education.
Credit unions are guided by a service-focused model. Their goal is not to create profits for outside owners. Their purpose is to provide:
Safe savings options
Reasonable borrowing choices
Helpful financial guidance
Many credit unions also invest in local education and community programs. These efforts may include:
Free Workshops and seminars
Financial coaching
Support for local organizations

Are Credit Union Funds Insured?
Federally insured credit unions use the National Credit Union Share Insurance Fund. The National Credit Union Administration manages the fund. Standard coverage is generally up to $250,000 for each qualifying account ownership category.
People often search for NCUA-insured credit unions when comparing safe places for deposits. Coverage may depend on:
Ownership type
Beneficiaries
Joint ownership
Retirement account structure
Deposit insurance helps protect qualifying funds if a federally insured credit union fails. Coverage does not protect investments such as:
Stocks
Bonds
Mutual funds
Certain annuities
Reviewing account ownership categories can help you understand how coverage applies.

Frequently Asked Questions
Can Joining a Credit Union Help You Build Credit?
Membership alone does not
raise your credit score
. Access to credit-building tools may help when used responsibly. A secured credit card, credit-builder loan, or small installment loan can create payment history.
On-time payments, low balances, and limited credit applications remain important. Ask whether payments are reported to all three major credit bureaus before choosing a product. Reviewing your credit report regularly can also help you find errors or areas that need attention.
Can You Keep Your Membership If You Move?
Moving away does not necessarily mean leaving your credit union membership behind. Many credit unions allow you to remain a Member after moving, provided your membership stays in good standing. Policies vary, so carefully review the membership agreement before relocating.
Digital services, shared branches, and ATM networks can make account access easier from another city or state. Address changes should be reported promptly to protect your account and communications. You should also check whether nearby fee-free ATMs or partner locations are available.
